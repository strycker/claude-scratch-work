What hedge funds actually do, mapped to your ideas

Your "conditional hidden Markov model where transitions depend on features" has a name in the econometrics literature: a Markov-switching model with time-varying transition probabilities (Hamilton 1989 for the base model; Filardo 1994 and Diebold–Lee–Weinbach for the TVTP extension). In ML language it's an input-output HMM. Macro funds and central banks use these constantly. The full version of your "everything is coupled differential equations" intuition is the Markov-switching VAR (Krolzig): a vector autoregression over your feature set where both the coefficients and the covariance matrix switch by regime. Your line "correlations hold until they don't" is literally the motivation for regime-dependent covariance — the empirical fact that equity-bond correlation flips sign across inflation regimes, and that all correlations go to one in a crisis. Ang and Bekaert have a classic line of papers on exactly your end goal: regime-switching models feeding international asset allocation.

Bridgewater's All Weather is worth knowing as a design reference because it's your 4-state intuition made explicit and deliberately not estimated: growth rising/falling × inflation rising/falling, four quadrants, hold assets that perform in each, weighted by risk. They chose a hand-specified regime structure over a learned one, and the reason why is the crux of this whole conversation.

So why isn't the learned version dominant at hedge funds? Three reasons, and only one of them is timescale.

First, statistical power. Monthly equity returns run roughly 0.8% mean against 4.5% vol; a predictive R² of 1% at monthly horizon is publishable (Campbell–Thompson), and Goyal–Welch showed most published predictors have negative out-of-sample R². Meanwhile your model class is parameter-hungry: K regimes gives you K covariance matrices, K sets of VAR coefficients, and a K×K transition matrix that's now also a function of features. Regimes are persistent, so your effective sample isn't 1,200 monthly observations — it's the number of regime transitions you've witnessed, which for post-war US data is maybe 15–30 genuinely independent episodes. You know exactly what happens when you fit a flexible supervised model to 25 effective data points.

Second, Grinold's fundamental law: IR ≈ IC × √breadth. Stat arb funds accept tiny per-bet skill because they make tens of thousands of bets. A regime-timing strategy makes perhaps a handful of meaningful bets per decade. To win with low breadth you need high IC per bet, and macro forecasting IC is notoriously low. That's the real reason quant funds concentrate at short horizons — not that long-horizon signals don't exist, but that breadth lives at short horizons.

Third — and this is your edge — institutional constraints. Valuation signals like CAPE and the Buffett Indicator genuinely predict returns at 5–10 year horizons but are nearly useless for timing within a year or three (the Buffett Indicator has been "flashing warnings" for over a decade). A fund manager who de-risks on valuation and then trails the benchmark for four years gets redeemed out of existence before the signal pays. You have no redemptions, no career risk, no capacity constraints, and tax control. Your structural advantage isn't a better model; it's the ability to actually hold the long-horizon positions the models justify. Asness's "sin a little" framing is the right one here: valuation-based timing should tilt allocations modestly, never gate them binarily.

Where your sketch is strong

Regime-conditioning is the right organizing principle for your timescale, full stop. The insistence on probabilistic state (soft regime probabilities, not a hard label) is correct and is exactly what your k-means version lacked — hard assignments near cluster boundaries flip-flop, and HMM temporal persistence fixes that. Thinking at the portfolio level rather than the pick level is right. And separate models per horizon is right, because the predictable component of returns comes from different sources at different horizons: momentum at 3–12 months, valuation at 5+ years, nearly nothing in between.

Where it's weak or dangerous

The big one: smoothed vs. filtered state estimates. When you fit an HMM and look at the regime sequence, you're seeing Viterbi/smoothed probabilities that use future data. The March 2009 bottom is obvious in the smoothed path; the filtered (real-time) probability was still screaming crisis. Every regime backtest must use filtered probabilities only, and macro features must be point-in-time (ALFRED, not FRED — unemployment and GDP get revised, and using revised series is silent lookahead). This single issue kills more regime strategies than everything else combined.

Second: the "predict the features from the state" loop has a circularity smell — the state was defined from those features. The clean formulation is the MS-VAR: state and feature dynamics are estimated jointly, and prediction is the model's forward simulation, not a bolted-on second stage.

Third: even with good regime probabilities, don't feed point forecasts into a mean-variance optimizer — optimizers are error maximizers (Michaud). You want the full predictive distribution (simulate forward through the transition matrix, mixture over regimes) feeding a robust allocation step: Black–Litterman to blend regime views with equilibrium weights, or hierarchical risk parity, with fractional-Kelly sizing. You already built a robust multi-asset optimizer for your oil thesis; that's the right instinct and it slots in as the final stage here.

Coupled dynamics are real but the noise term dominates the drift term at every horizon you care about — the honest formalism is stochastic (regime-switching SDEs or discrete-time VAR with fat-tailed innovations), and the deterministic coupling coefficients themselves drift as the economy's structure changes.

Filtered vs. smoothed: two layers of lookahead

When you fit an HMM via EM/Baum-Welch and inspect the regime path, you're looking at smoothed probabilities P(S_t | data through T) — the forward-backward algorithm explicitly uses the future to clean up the past. The filtered probability P(S_t | data through t) is what you'd actually have known at time t, and the difference is not cosmetic. In a typical two-to-four state equity model, the smoothed path shows crisp regime blocks with clean edges; the filtered path shows the same blocks but with ragged, delayed onsets and hesitant exits. The March 2009 example generalizes: smoothed paths "call the bottom" because the backward pass knows the recovery happened. A backtest on smoothed states will show regime timing that looks like genius and is actually time travel.

But there's a second, sneakier layer: parameter lookahead. Even if you virtuously use filtered probabilities, if the model parameters (transition matrix, regime means, covariances) were estimated on the full sample, then your 1995 filtered probability was computed with knowledge of what regimes look like through 2025 — including regimes that hadn't been discovered yet in 1995. The 2008 crisis regime's covariance signature is baked into parameters used to filter 1995 data. The honest protocol is walk-forward: at each rebalance date t, re-estimate the model on data through t only, compute the filtered probability, record the allocation decision, step forward. At monthly rebalancing over a 60-year history that's ~700 model fits, which is trivially cheap for an HMM — a few minutes total. There is no computational excuse to skip this, and the performance gap between the lazy backtest and the honest one is your measurement of how much of the strategy was hindsight. That gap is worth reporting as a first-class metric.

A corollary metric worth building in: detection lag — for each historical regime transition (identified ex post via smoothing), how many periods until the filtered probability crossed your action threshold? For monthly macro regimes this is typically 1–3 months. If your regimes have average sojourns of 18 months and detection lag is 2, you capture most of the regime; if sojourns are 5 months, the lag eats the trade. This single ratio — sojourn duration to detection lag — largely determines whether regime timing can work at all at a given granularity, and it's a much more honest diagnostic than backtest Sharpe.

Related: filtered probabilities near a transition oscillate, and if allocations respond linearly you'll churn. Standard fix is hysteresis — act when P(regime) crosses 0.7, unwind when it falls below 0.4, do nothing in between — or smooth the allocation response so turnover is bounded. For an investor-not-trader this matters doubly, since your transaction costs include taxes.

Point-in-time discipline

Macro series are revised, sometimes drastically. Real-time GDP growth readings have been revised by full percentage points; early-2008 vintage data showed mild slowdown while the final revisions show recession already underway. Payrolls, GDP, and industrial production all revise; using today's FRED series means your 2008 features contain information nobody had in 2008. ALFRED (ArchivaL FRED) preserves vintages — you query "what did the June 2008 unemployment rate look like as published in July 2008." It's the same St. Louis Fed infrastructure you already use, just with a vintage dimension.

Publication lag compounds this: CPI arrives mid-following-month, GDP quarterly with a month's delay and two subsequent revisions. Your feature matrix at date t must only contain values published by t, not merely referring to periods before t.

The practical design consequence — and this is a genuine architecture decision worth recording — is a strong preference for market-observed features over statistical-agency features. Yield curve slope, credit spreads (BAA-AAA or high-yield OAS), realized volatility, trailing asset-class returns, oil, gold, the dollar, breakevens (TIPS-implied inflation): all observable in real time, never revised, no vintage headaches. And conveniently, markets themselves aggregate macro information faster than the agencies publish it — the curve and credit spreads are forward-looking regime indicators, while unemployment is a lagging one. A defensible design uses market features for the fast regime-detection layer and reserves agency data (and slow valuation anchors like CAPE and the Buffett Indicator, where revision noise is small relative to the signal's decade-scale swing) for the slow strategic-tilt layer. Your trading-crab feature set mixes both categories freely, so this is a concrete re-evaluation item.

One more leakage channel specific to your supervised component: overlapping forward-return labels. If you label each month with the subsequent 12-month return, adjacent labels share 11 months of data and are ~92% correlated. Standard CV — even TimeSeriesSplit — leaks, because training observations near the test boundary contain the test period's returns inside their labels. López de Prado's fix is purging (drop training samples whose label windows overlap the test window) plus an embargo gap. Advances in Financial Machine Learning chapters 7–8 are the reference; your existing TimeSeriesSplit in trading-crab needs this amendment before any CV number from it can be trusted.

Topic two: how many regimes, and are they real?

Statistical criteria will betray you here. BIC and AIC on financial data reliably favor more states, because extra states get spent absorbing outliers and fat tails rather than discovering economically distinct environments — a "regime" containing October 1987, September 2008, and March 2020 isn't a market condition, it's a t-distribution being approximated by mixture components. Two mitigations: use fat-tailed emissions (Student-t rather than Gaussian) so the tails don't masquerade as regimes, and prefer ICL (integrated completed likelihood) over BIC, since it penalizes states that don't cleanly separate.

But the stronger tests are stability and interpretability, and I'd elevate these to acceptance criteria for the build. Stability: re-estimate on subsamples (drop the first decade, drop the last, bootstrap blocks) and check the regimes persist — same rough means, covariances, and sojourn structure. Beware label switching (state 2 in one fit is state 4 in another); match states by emission parameters, e.g. Hungarian algorithm on distribution distances, before comparing. If a regime appears in the full-sample fit but evaporates when you drop 2008–2009, it's an episode, not a regime. Interpretability: each state should have a one-line economic description a portfolio manager would nod at, sojourn durations of months-to-years (a "regime" lasting three weeks at monthly granularity is noise), and — critically for your purposes — regimes must differ in the conditional distribution of asset returns, not just in feature space. A regime that's statistically distinct in macro features but implies the same portfolio is useless to you. That suggests validating regime count against out-of-sample allocation value, not likelihood.

Where this lands, given the effective-sample problem from last time: 4 regimes is defensible, 5–6 is the ceiling, and 10 is a mirage — with maybe 25 independent regime transitions in the post-war record, a 10-state model has ~90 transition parameters before you've conditioned on features. This is also where the hybrid design earns its keep: hand-specify the regime skeleton (e.g., the growth×inflation quadrants plus a crisis state — crisis deserves its own state because its covariance signature, correlations→1, is unlike anything else) by constraining the sign structure of emission means, then let estimation fill in the magnitudes, covariances, and feature-dependent transitions. You keep the discovery where data can support it (dynamics, distributions) and impose structure where it can't (state semantics). It also solves label-switching by construction and makes every downstream output human-readable for free. The fully unsupervised version you built in trading-crab is the right exploratory tool — use it to check whether the data wants states your skeleton lacks — but I'd argue against it as the production spine.

The fine print on hindsight labels, then I'll drop it

"Label with all information, predict with causal features" is a recognized pattern (López de Prado's labeling chapters are in this spirit, and regime-classification papers from practitioners do exactly this). Three residual cautions, all operational rather than philosophical. First, the labels near the end of your sample are unstable by construction — smoothing needs future data, so the last ~6–12 months of labels will keep revising as new data arrives. Embargo that tail from training, and track "label churn" as a monitoring metric; if your labeler keeps reclassifying 2024 as new data arrives, that instability propagates into everything downstream. Second, when you evaluate the supervised model, the honest headline number is accuracy near transitions, not overall — a persistence-dominated label sequence lets a classifier score 90% by predicting "same as yesterday." Report transition-window performance separately. Third, your causal feature set should use one-sided filters only (EMAs, backward-looking derivatives) — centered or zero-phase smoothing (filtfilt-style) silently reintroduces the future. You know this; recording it so it lands in the design doc.

One genuinely nice trick your framing enables: the smoothed posteriors γ_t(k) give you label confidence, not just labels. Use them as sample weights in the supervised stage — periods where the smoother was itself ambiguous (γ split 55/45) get downweighted, so your classifier isn't forced to learn crisp boundaries the ground truth never had.

On balance: right instinct, wrong mechanism

Don't force balance in the clustering step — you built balanced k-means with bipartite matching for exactly this kind of thing, and I'd specifically warn against reaching for it here, because equal-occupancy states on market data are statistically artificial: the market genuinely spends 70–80% of months in expansion. But your underlying complaint is correct — one fat "bull" state is useless — and the fix is to split the dominant regime along axes where conditional return distributions actually differ, which they do. The literature robustly finds at least two distinct expansion types: low-volatility grind (the best Sharpe environment in the data) versus high-volatility late-cycle expansion (positive drift, fat left tail, momentum crashes live here). Add a recovery/rebound state — post-crisis periods have the highest returns in the sample, huge vol, and violent junk-quality rallies, a conditional distribution unlike calm bull — and occupancy starts spreading naturally. A defensible 6-state skeleton: calm expansion, volatile/late-cycle expansion, inflationary expansion (your stagflation-adjacent state), disinflationary slowdown, crisis/deleveraging, recovery. Rather than a balance constraint, use an occupancy floor as an acceptance criterion: no state below ~8% of months, no state above ~35%. That gets you "roughly balanced" as an emergent property of good state definitions instead of a distortion imposed on the geometry.

The Markov machinery — what you actually need to know

Here's the compact version, oriented toward what's load-bearing for your design.

Structure and the two recursions. An HMM is: hidden state sequence S_t ∈ {1..K} following a Markov chain with transition matrix A, plus emission distributions b_k(x_t) = p(x_t | S_t = k). Everything you care about comes from two recursions. The forward pass computes α_t(j) = p(x_{1:t}, S_t = j) via α_t(j) = [Σ_i α_{t-1}(i) A_ij] · b_j(x_t); normalizing α_t gives the filtered probability — the real-time state estimate. The backward pass computes β_t(i) = p(x_{t+1:T} | S_t = i), and the product γ_t(i) ∝ α_t(i)β_t(i) is the smoothed posterior — this is your ground-truth generator. So filtered-vs-smoothed isn't two models; it's one model read with and without the backward pass. Complexity is O(TK²) — nothing here is ever slow at your scale.

Decoding: γ vs. Viterbi. Two ways to extract a label sequence from the smoothed model, and they differ in a way that matters for you. Taking argmax of γ_t at each t independently maximizes per-point accuracy but can produce transition sequences the chain considers near-impossible (state 2 → state 5 for one month when A_25 ≈ 0). Viterbi finds the single most probable path, which respects transition structure and gives cleaner, more persistent label blocks. For ground-truth labeling, use Viterbi for the hard labels and keep γ alongside for the confidence weights. This choice alone substantially affects how choppy your labels are.

Estimation: Baum-Welch is just EM, and the M-step is your day job. E-step runs forward-backward to get γ_t(i) (state occupancies) and ξ_t(i,j) (expected transitions). M-step: for Gaussian emissions, closed-form weighted means/covariances; for the transition matrix, normalized ξ counts. Two practical warnings. EM on HMMs is riddled with local optima — always multi-restart, and a natural warm start is your existing trading-crab k-means clusters (init emission parameters from cluster moments, init A near-diagonal). And near-singular solutions (a state collapsing onto a handful of points, covariance → 0) need either covariance regularization or a prior; hmmlearn will happily hand you degenerate fits without complaint.

TVTP — the "conditional" part of your conditional HMM. Replace the constant A with a softmax: P(S_t = j | S_{t-1} = i, z_t) ∝ exp(a_ij + β_j′ z_t), where z_t is your feature vector (curve slope, credit spreads, vol, momentum...). The elegant part for you: EM still applies, and the M-step for the transition parameters becomes a weighted multinomial logistic regression with the ξ_t(i,j) as fractional targets — literally a classification problem, which means everything you know about regularizing and validating classifiers imports directly. That's the deep connection between your two-stage instinct and the joint model: a TVTP-HMM is your supervised transition model, just estimated jointly with the labels instead of after them. Filardo (1994) and Diebold–Lee–Weinbach are the original references; Kim & Nelson's State-Space Models with Regime Switching is the book I'd actually recommend you buy — it's the standard reference and written for exactly your profile.

The geometric-duration problem — this is the biggest hidden assumption. A first-order Markov chain forces sojourn times to be geometric: P(stay k periods) = A_ii^(k-1)(1−A_ii), memoryless, mode at 1. Real regime durations aren't — expansions age, and hazard rates aren't constant. Three escalating fixes: (1) ignore it, often fine at monthly granularity for labeling purposes; (2) hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) with explicit duration distributions (negative binomial works well) — more parameters, better shaped sojourns; (3) the cheap trick — include "time in current regime" as a feature in z_t in the TVTP softmax, which lets the exit hazard depend on regime age without HSMM machinery. Given your architecture, (3) is nearly free and I'd default to it. In your decoupled design the analogue is simply including regime-age as a feature in the supervised transition model — same trick, zero extra math.

Also: your two supervised targets are different problems. Predicting S_t from causal features through t is nowcasting — replacing the HMM filter with a discriminative classifier. Predicting S_{t+1} (or the transition indicator) is the transition model. Keep them separate; they have different label geometries, different feature relevances (spreads and vol lead transitions; levels identify states), and different evaluation protocols. And in the nowcaster, include the prior period's predicted state (or predicted distribution) as a feature — otherwise you've thrown away the persistence structure that makes the problem tractable, and you'll get flickering predictions that a recursive structure would have suppressed.

One serious alternative for your labeling stage: jump models

Given that you've decoupled labeling from prediction, the labeler doesn't have to be an HMM at all, and there's a strong recent literature saying it maybe shouldn't be. The statistical jump model (Bemporad/Boyd 2018; Nystrup, Lindström & Madsen applied it to regimes) minimizes Σ_t ‖x_t − μ_{s_t}‖² + λ·Σ_t 1[s_t ≠ s_{t−1}] — i.e., k-means plus a per-jump penalty. Solved by alternating: given centroids, the optimal state sequence is exact dynamic programming (Viterbi-like, O(TK²)); given states, centroids are means. It's k-means with temporal persistence bolted on — which is precisely the conceptual upgrade from your trading-crab clustering — and the empirical findings are that jump-model labels are markedly more stable out-of-sample than HMM labels (less sensitive to re-estimation, less label churn at the sample edge, which directly addresses the embargo problem above). And λ is a single interpretable knob that directly controls persistence and, indirectly, occupancy balance — tune it until sojourns and occupancy hit your acceptance criteria. There's a continuous-state extension where s_t is a probability vector, giving you soft labels for the confidence-weighting trick. My honest recommendation for your architecture: jump model for stage-1 ground truth, gradient-boosted or logistic nowcaster/transition models for stage 2, and keep the TVTP-HMM as the benchmark to beat rather than the spine. You get the Markov structure where it earns its keep (the DP in the labeler, persistence features in the classifiers) without inheriting EM's fragility.

What you described is a mixture of experts, and the literature agrees with you

What AVWAP actually is, mathematically and behaviorally

AVWAP from anchor τ is just AVWAP_τ(t) = Σ_{s=τ..t} p_s·v_s / Σ_{s=τ..t} v_s — the volume-weighted average price of every share traded since the anchor event. Its interpretation is what makes it interesting: it's the average cost basis of every participant who entered since τ. Price above the anchor's AVWAP means the average post-event entrant is in profit; below means underwater. That gives it two real transmission mechanisms rather than chart mysticism: the disposition effect (underwater holders cluster sell orders near their breakeven — "bag holders exiting at even" is the retail description of a documented behavioral bias), and institutional execution benchmarking — funds literally benchmark large executions against VWAP (Almgren–Chriss, Madhavan are the execution-cost literature), so VWAP-anchored levels are where institutional limit orders actually rest, making the levels partially self-fulfilling. Among technical indicators, this one has unusually defensible microfoundations — better than most of the TA canon.

The extension that fits your platform like a glove: algorithmic anchor selection

AVWAP's known weakness is that anchor choice is subjective — Shannon anchors at swing highs/lows, earnings gaps, news candles, chosen by eye. Your platform removes the subjectivity, and this is where your "extend Silva to my horizon" goal gets concrete, because you have something no discretionary trader has: a regime engine that generates anchors systematically. Anchor a VWAP at the date the current regime began (from L1 labels historically, L2 detection in real time), and you get "the average cost basis of everyone positioned since this market environment started" — a regime-conditional fair-value reference that persists for months, exactly matching your holding periods. That's a genuine marriage of the two frameworks, and I haven't seen it published in this form. The full systematic anchor family: regime-transition dates, 52-week high/low dates, earnings dates (per single name), calendar institutional benchmarks (year/quarter opens — funds anchor there), and volume-percentile days (top-decile volume days are event candles by definition, detected mechanically). Every anchor is rule-generated, which means the whole thing backtests honestly — no hindsight anchor-picking, which is the silent leakage in most discretionary AVWAP analysis.

Options as an information source, not an instrument

This is a well-trodden and legitimate move — the options market is a forecast market, and you can consume its forecasts without ever touching a contract. The key fact that makes this practical for you: the asset-class level of this information is free with full history, because CBOE publishes it as indices. VIX is 30-day SPX implied vol; VIX9D/VIX3M/VIX6M give you the term structure; VVIX is vol-of-vol; SKEW prices the tail; RVX covers small caps; OVX and GVZ are literally "the VIX of oil" and "the VIX of gold" — your two favorite commodities have their own implied-vol series. So your data constraint mostly evaporates at the asset-class layer, which is exactly where your platform lives.

Silva's "expected move" demystified: it's the straddle-implied range, and you can compute it from the free indices without any chain data — expected move ≈ S · (IV/100) · √(T/365), and his 2σ levels are S·(1 ± 2·IV·√T). For SPY that's just spot and VIX. What he computes per-ticker from chains, you can compute per-asset-class from indices, historically, backtestably.

Now the features worth building, in order of expected value. First, the variance risk premium: VRP = implied variance − realized variance (VIX² − RV²). This is one of the better-documented return predictors in the literature (Bollerslev–Tauchen–Zhou 2009): high VRP — the market paying up for insurance relative to actual turbulence — predicts positive equity returns at your exact horizons (1–6 months), with R² that embarrasses most predictors. It's also conceptually clean in your language: it's the wedge between the risk-neutral (Q) and physical (P) distributions, i.e., the price of fear, and the price of fear mean-reverts. Second, IV as an input to the vol layer: implied vol is a biased-but-informative forecast of realized vol (the bias is the VRP), and blending IV with GARCH demonstrably improves vol forecasts — so §6.2 should ingest the VIX family directly, and OVX/GVZ give your oil and gold vol targeting forward-looking inputs. Third, term structure and SKEW as crash-module features, which we already logged in §16.3 — this strengthens that case.

The per-ticker chain-level stuff — open-interest "volume shelves," gamma exposure (GEX), max pain — is where data gets expensive and history gets unobtainable. Honest verdict: OI concentrations do act as short-term pinning/magnet levels via dealer delta-hedging flows, but without history you can't backtest them, so they can't be features. What they can be is levels: Fidelity gives you current chains free, so a small utility that reads the current chain and marks the big OI strikes gives you Silva-style reference levels for sleeve entries/exits — used the way you'd use a support line, with no pretense of statistical validation. Right tool, right epistemic status. One more chain-derived idea for the someday-list: Breeden–Litzenberger (1978) shows the full risk-neutral density is the second derivative of call price with respect to strike — the options market hands you an entire predictive distribution per expiry, which connects to your distributional-forecast open question, but it needs chain history you don't have. The finance-to-math dictionary, and whether you've been reinventing wheels

Short answer: you have been, several times, and that's good news — it's convergent validity, and naming the wheels unlocks their literatures. The full table goes in the doc; here are the punchlines. Beta is nothing but an OLS coefficient: Cov(r_i, r_m)/Var(r_m), the slope of asset excess returns on market excess returns. Your L3b factor-projection layer is multivariate beta estimation; CAPM is just the claim that one regressor suffices (it doesn't; Fama–French is the admission). Alpha is the intercept — mean residual return after controlling for exposures — and "finding alpha" is literally "E[ε] > 0 with a defensible t-stat." Sharpe ratio is a t-statistic in disguise: annualized standardized mean excess return; multiply by √years and you have the t-stat, which is exactly why track-record length dominates skill inference and why the deflated Sharpe exists. IC is a correlation (forecast vs. outcome), and Grinold's law IR ≈ IC·√breadth is just how correlation aggregates over independent bets. The Greeks are Taylor-expansion terms — delta/gamma are first/second derivatives in price, vega in vol, theta in time; bond duration and convexity are the identical expansion in yield. Nothing in the vocabulary is mathematically new to you; it's all regression, moments, derivatives, and quantiles wearing suits.

And your specific reinventions, mapped: your fair-value gap is the value factor (deviation from a slow anchor); your regime-conditional coefficients are conditional betas (there's a whole conditional-CAPM literature); the MoE is a conditional factor model. But the mapping pays forward too — two documented effects fall out of the dictionary as near-free model candidates you didn't have. Residual momentum (Blitz–Huij–Martens): momentum computed on the factor-regression residuals ε rather than raw returns delivers a better Sharpe than classic momentum with smaller crashes — and since L3b already computes the residuals, this feature costs you one line. And betting-against-beta (Frazzini–Pedersen): low-beta assets persistently deliver higher risk-adjusted returns than CAPM says (leverage-constrained investors overpay for high beta) — directly relevant to long-only sizing, since it says tilting toward boring low-beta names and sizing up beats holding glamour high-beta names, which harmonizes with your vol-targeting layer.

Volume: you're right, and there's real theory under the instinct

Volume is underrated by retail and heavily used by quants — just not in the OBV-style heuristic forms. The theoretical anchor is Clark's Mixture of Distributions Hypothesis (1973): volume proxies the rate of information arrival, and returns are best modeled as subordinated to an information clock rather than the calendar clock — high-volume periods are "more time passing" in market terms. That's the deep version of why López de Prado advocates dollar bars over time bars. For your monthly spine the practical descendants are: volume-surprise features (log volume relative to its own EMA — raw volume has secular growth and must be detrended; turnover or dollar volume normalized by trailing average), which belong in the regime emissions — regime shifts are information-arrival shifts, and volume surges are precisely your §17.2 event-anchor trigger generalized into a continuous feature. Second, Amihud illiquidity (|return|/dollar volume): illiquidity is a priced factor and it spikes ahead of and during crises, making it a legitimate regime-detection input — liquidity evaporation is arguably the defining mechanical feature of your crisis state. Third — and this one is directly a coefficient in your differential-equation layer — Campbell–Grossman–Wang (1993): price moves on high volume tend to continue; price moves on low volume tend to revert. That's an interaction term, return × volume-surprise, that modulates the continuation-vs-reversion coefficient — which means volume belongs in the MoE not just as a feature but as a gate on the sign of the autocorrelation structure, which is about as native to your "coefficients depend on conditions" architecture as anything could be. It also gives the fair-value gap module a refinement: gaps opened on low volume should revert more reliably than gaps opened on high volume.

So volume enters at four points: regime emissions (volume-surprise, Amihud), transition/nowcast features (liquidity deterioration leads stress), MoE interactions (CGW continuation/reversion gate), and the already-volume-weighted AVWAP module. Updating the doc.

No-trade bands: the math

The theory (Constantinides 1986, Davis–Norman 1990, multi-asset Leland 2000): the utility loss from deviating from target is second-order — L ≈ ½γ(w−w*)′Σ(w−w*), quadratic in the deviation — while trading costs are first-order (linear). Quadratic benefit vs. linear cost means the optimal policy is never continuous rebalancing but a no-trade region around w*, and the classic result is the cube-root law: band half-width δ ∝ (c/γ)^(1/3). The cube root is the punchline — even tiny costs justify surprisingly wide bands, which is why continuous rebalancing is always wrong. Since your frictions are deliberately out of scope, the elegant reinterpretation for your platform is: the "cost" is estimation noise, and the band is a confidence interval on w*. Your target weights are estimates with standard errors (bootstrap/resample the inputs — Michaud resampling — to get SE(w*)); trading a deviation smaller than the noise in the target itself is trading randomness. Spec: dual trigger — per-asset band |w_i − w_i| > max(floor, z·SE(w_i)), scaled up for high-vol and down for diversifying assets, or a portfolio-level tracking-error trigger (w−w*)′Σ(w−w*) > τ² to catch many-small-deviations cases; on trigger, trade to the band edge (not the center — the half-adjustment result), prioritized by utility gain per unit turnover. That's the complete weekly action-generation math, and it's a few dozen lines of code.

(K, λ) selection protocol for the jump model

Grid: K ∈ {4,5,6,7} × λ on a log grid (λ interacts with K — more states need a stiffer jump penalty to keep sojourns long — and should be normalized by feature dimension so the grid transfers across feature-set changes). For each cell, fit with multi-restart, then evaluate in two stages. Stage A, hard acceptance filters (§4.4 operationalized): occupancy in [8%, 35%]; median sojourn ≥ some months-scale floor; skeleton mappability (each state matches one semantic slot via its emission signature, Hungarian-matched); and stability, measured concretely as ARI/AMI agreement between full-sample labels and leave-one-decade-out labels, plus online–offline agreement (labels from expanding-window refits vs. final labels — this one directly predicts how much label churn you'll suffer live, which Nystrup et al. found is where jump models beat HMMs). Reconciled jump model mechanics, formalized multi-timescale consensus framework, elevated tripwire orchestrator
Three things to untangle here — one confusion to clear up (with a physics mapping you'll appreciate), one genuinely important design principle to formalize, and one honest "I can't find that indicator."

The jump model: you've conflated two layers, but your physics instinct is exactly right

The jump model is not the rebalancing mechanism — it's the Layer 1 ground-truth labeler, the replacement for your balanced k-means. The rebalance-only-past-a-threshold machinery is the no-trade band layer (§21), which lives at the opposite end of the pipeline. But here's why the confusion is forgivable and even insightful: both are the same principle — penalize state changes so that only persistent evidence moves you — applied at different layers. And your magnetism instinct is not an analogy, it's an identity. Look at the jump model objective again:

J(s, μ) = Σ_t ‖x_t − μ_{s_t}‖² + λ·Σ_t 1[s_t ≠ s_{t−1}]

That is, term for term, the energy of a one-dimensional K-state Potts model: the first term is a site-dependent external field (how badly each month's data fits each candidate state), and the second is a ferromagnetic nearest-neighbor coupling of strength λ on a chain of spins s_t ∈ {1..K}. Finding the optimal label sequence is finding the ground state of the Potts chain, which is why dynamic programming solves it exactly — 1D chains are exactly solvable, same reason the transfer matrix works. λ is the domain-wall energy: each regime transition costs λ, so large λ produces long magnetic domains, which are your persistent regimes. The HMM slots into the same picture one step up: it's the finite-temperature version — log A_ij are the couplings, the smoothed posteriors γ_t(k) are Boltzmann marginals, forward-backward is the transfer matrix, and Viterbi is the zero-temperature ground state. So when I said earlier "jump model preferred, HMM as benchmark," in your native language that's "ground-state decode preferred, finite-temperature marginals as benchmark, with γ as the local Boltzmann weights we reuse for confidence."

And hysteresis proper — path-dependence, not just stickiness — appears at a different layer: the L2 action bands (enter defensive posture when P(crisis) crosses 0.7, exit only below 0.4). That's literally a hysteresis loop: the system's state depends on which direction you approached from, exactly like magnetization lagging the applied field. So the full picture is three stickiness mechanisms at three layers: domain-wall energy in the labels (λ), hysteresis in the regime-triggered actions (probability bands), and a no-trade region in the weights (§21). Each suppresses churn from a different noise source, and none substitutes for the others.

Multi-timescale consensus: yes, and it needs one statistical warning label

Your "act when horizons agree" principle is validated practice — trend-following funds run ensembles of lookback speeds rather than single filters (the time-series momentum literature, Moskowitz–Ooi–Pedersen, explicitly combines 1m/3m/12m signals), and Shannon's book is titled Multiple Timeframes for the same reason. Reconciling stop-loss contradictions with position sizing fundamentals
Let me check what happened with gold this morning for context first.


Examined gap-risk mechanics and reconsidered stop-loss strategy fundamentals
You got gapped through your stop — the phenomenon is called gap risk, and the execution shortfall (sold ~3% down on a 1% stop) is slippage. The mechanics: a plain stop-loss is a trigger, not a guarantee — the moment price touches your stop level, it converts into a market order, which fills at the next available price. When the ETF opens 3% below yesterday's close, "the next available price" is 3% down, and your 1% stop delivers a 3% loss. Worse, as you experienced, it delivers that loss at exactly the price where you'd have been a buyer.

And your gold position was structurally set up for this, which is worth understanding because it generalizes: gold trades nearly 24 hours in the spot and futures markets, but IAU/GLD only trade during US equity hours. Overnight, gold fell from around $4,168 to roughly $4,076 — spot was at $4,076 by 9:00 a.m. ET today versus $4,168 at the same time Tuesday, on a stronger dollar and the 10-year Treasury yield pushing back to ~4.5% — so the entire move happened while your stop couldn't execute at all, then materialized as an opening gap the instant it could. Any ETF whose underlying trades overnight (gold, oil, international equities) carries this exposure permanently: your stop only lives during the 6.5 hours the ETF trades, while the risk lives around the clock. It also happened on a scheduled event day — FOMC minutes drop today at 2:00 p.m. ET, from a committee that split nine-to-nine on 2026 rate hikes — and gaps cluster around exactly these macro release windows.
CNBC + 2

The order type you're asking for exists: it's a stop-limit order. Instead of one price you set two — a stop (trigger) price and a limit price — and when the trigger hits, it places a limit order rather than a market order. Set the stop at −1% with a limit at −1.5%, and a gap to −3% blows straight past your limit and you simply don't sell. That's precisely your "conditional stop-loss range," and Fidelity supports it on ETFs. But name the trade-off honestly, because it's real: the stop-limit protects you from selling into gaps at the cost of not protecting you at all when protection matters most. In a genuine crash — the grinding kind you built this whole platform to escape — price gaps through your limit and keeps falling, and you're still holding. The plain stop guarantees exit but not price; the stop-limit guarantees price but not exit. There is no order type that guarantees both; the only instrument that truly caps gap risk is a protective put, which you've reasonably ruled out. So the answer isn't an order type — it's the following stack of tactics.

First, your stop was mis-sized relative to volatility, independent of the gap. A 1% stop on gold right now sits inside daily noise — gold's dailies have been running 1–2% routinely (it's down 7% year-to-date after a rollercoaster from above $5,500 in late January to below $4,000 in June, and Q2 was its worst quarter since 2013). A stop inside one daily sigma isn't crash protection; it's a coin-flip exit on any given day. This is exactly the §7 spec in your design doc meeting reality: stop distance should be a multiple of forecast vol — 2–3× ATR is the standard practitioner form — so in the current regime a gold stop belongs at 3–5% out, not 1%.
World Gold Council

Second — and this is the real risk control — size the position, not the stop. Hold dollar risk constant: position size = (risk budget per position) ÷ (stop distance). If you want to risk 1% of the portfolio on gold, a 4% stop means a 25% smaller position than a 1% stop would allow — and crucially, a gap's damage scales with position size, not stop tightness. Tight stop + large position is maximally exposed to gaps; wide stop + small position takes the same planned risk and is robust to them. Tightening the stop to compensate for an oversized position is the trap.

Third, consider close-based rules instead of resting intraday stops. Many systematic trend followers don't rest stop orders at all: the rule is "if it closes below X, sell tomorrow." This immunizes you against intraday spikes and stop-running, converts the gap-at-open problem into a considered next-day decision, and — most relevantly for you — the alert-based version fits your platform philosophy perfectly. You've said nothing is automated and you execute all trades yourself; a resting stop order is, in fact, an automated trade sitting at your broker. The design-consistent alternative: Fidelity price alerts at your model-derived stop levels, and you decide at the close — with the tripwire monitor's context in front of you — whether the touch was noise, a gap to fade, or a regime signal to honor. Slower by hours, smarter by a lot, and consistent with a weekly-cadence investor.

Fourth, the deepest lesson: your stop contradicted your thesis. You said it yourself — at −3% you'd have bought. That means lower prices made gold more attractive to you, which means your position was a value/reversion-flavored holding, and stops are the wrong tool for reversion theses entirely. A stop encodes "below this price, my thesis is invalidated." For a trend thesis that's coherent — trend broken, get out. For a reversion or strategic-ballast thesis, lower prices confirm the entry logic rather than invalidate it, so the correct risk tools are position sizing and pre-committed tranche buying (your cost-averaging protocol from §23.4), not an exit trigger that mechanically sells you the dip you wanted to buy. This is the regime-conditional stop logic from §7 restated at the thesis level: tight stops for trend positions, wide-or-no stops plus small sizing for reversion positions. This morning you ran a trend-position risk tool on a reversion-position thesis, and it did exactly what that mismatch does.

One process note, gently: a stop set to trigger on a 1% move against a 3–5% expected adverse range was inverted from the start — the stop must sit outside the noise you're willing to endure and inside only what falsifies the position. And the standard disclaimers apply — I'm not a financial advisor and the sizing choices are yours — but the mechanics above are standard practice, not opinion.
